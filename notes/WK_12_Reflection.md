### Reflection

Reflecting on the project, I'm as glad for what I didn't do as for what I did. The things I'm actually good at — modelling, materials, lighting — are almost comically absent here; there isn't even any UV mapping. The models are crude, and I left them that way on purpose. A detailed, polished environment-art piece would have fought the very layered visual language of anxiety I was trying to build. Flexing those skills would have made the wrong piece.

When I started, my instinct was to make something "cool" as a way to explore new tools (Unreal, Blender geometry nodes) and say "look what I made." In the age of AI that impulse feels less relevant than ever. Being exposed to Simon Penny, to Bridget Riley's optical work, and to other students' thinking pushed me past it, toward actually asking what it means for someone to engage with a digital artwork, but more importantly why.

The project changed dramatically in form, but many of the core ideas stayed surprisingly constant — contrast, pattern density, Ikeda, anxiety. Discoveries along the way kept circling back to the original intent, even as I kept asking myself: what is the intent? The Ikeda-derived blips survived as the beeps and squares of a supermarket: utility-driven, not frightening black and white, a register beep that is supposed to grab your attention. Then against that in the periphy sits the radial banding of anxiety: more human, more circular, more pressure in the environment but not quite of it. The zebra striping makes the floor feel like it's dropping away; you can't anchor. It isn't sad so much as discombobulating — the anxiety turning "dirty" and overwhelming.

That constant question — what is the intent? — kept dragging me, a little reluctantly, into examining my own anxiety. It also made me notice how little thought I'd ever given to my own surroundings, which is a strange thing for a human: that we move through our environments, physical or otherwise, without really stopping to question them. The fluorescent lighting and the unwritten contract of behaviour in these places have always stirred a quiet discomfort in me. Having a personal relationship to anxiety is a hard thing to talk about — mental health is something I'm quick to empathise with in others and quick to scold in myself. If the piece prompts even one person to think "hey, I have this too" — quietly, tucked away in their pocket after the demonstration, that would be a win.

I hope it can open up a little more dialogue about mental health, especially as the pressures of modernity only seem to grow louder. As humans we ought to think about our environment and about joyful connection — otherwise, what is the point of any of it?
